# Psychology and cognition behind design decisions

Open this when a screen feels wrong but you cannot say why, when choosing how many options, items or steps to show, when designing trust, onboarding, billing, completion or retention moments, or when asked to "increase engagement". Each entry ties a human limit or bias to a concrete UI change. Studies are quoted as the speakers cite them, not independently checked.

## Checks to run before shipping
### List three objections and answer each on screen
Put yourself in the target user's seat and ask what would bother or not suit them (an eco-conscious buyer wants recyclable materials and ingredient info visible). List three objections and check the page answers each visibly, without a click. Then invert the tone once (bold and simple instead of elegant and exclusive) to find a fresher concept, if the audience still fits.
- Look for: an objection that only the founder can answer, or one buried below the fold.
- Fix: move the answer next to the CTA or the price it concerns.
Sources: SatoriGraphics ([Graphic Design FULL Course](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nKoNZ1u21ss)); SelfMadeWebDesigner ([A Web Designer's Journey with Mental Illness](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E3JiTz-RwpU))

### Write what the user knows, wants and fears at each step
Before designing a flow, write three lines per step: knows, wants, fears. Add the context next to each feature (device, interruption, urgency): a cashier in a busy supermarket and one in a quiet cafe have different brain states for the same job. Check copy and controls against those lines.
- Look for: labels that use your internal words, or a step that assumes context the user lacks at that moment.
Sources: SelfMadeWebDesigner ([A Web Designer's Journey with Mental Illness](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E3JiTz-RwpU)); amirmoradiUX ([The Real Reason Most Apps Get Abandoned](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H4cWT9SzR2E)); amirmoradiUX ([How to Become a Product Designer in the AI Era](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ky_F3jdnT4Y))

### Test with strangers, because you know too much
Curse of knowledge: the site makes sense to you because you built it, and UX is the invisible instruction manual. Write UI copy as if the user has zero context, and test with at least a few people unlike you. Watch for choices you only like because you made them. For a brand mark, show it to 5 people for 5 seconds and ask what the product is. If they cannot say, simplify or make it more relevant.
- Fix: validate that the problem and context are real with users before polishing.
Sources: SelfMadeWebDesigner ([How to Be A Web Designer in 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vYvzOyTA2z4)); Hatch Conference ([Jason Giles - Leading with Empathy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1CeXDNJTHXE)); SatoriGraphics ([4 Principles For PROFESSIONAL Logo Design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w0M3Z3oTh08))

### Name 1-3 emotions per page and the 3 moments that matter
Step 1: identify the audience, what they want and why. Step 2: pick 1-3 emotional triggers (craving, awe, exploration, trust, fear of missing out). Step 3: decide how layout, color, type, shape and imagery produce them. In an app emotion does not arrive on its own, so list the 3 highest-value moments in the flow (first success, a milestone, a trust-sensitive step) and give each a deliberate treatment in copy, motion and visual. Write the audience as one line and check each visual decision against it.
Sources: SatoriGraphics ([Advanced Graphic Design On REAL EXAMPLES](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8TzzCHyZY0k)); TimGabe ([How Addictive Apps Are Made](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AnL49w4yIOw)); SatoriGraphics ([6 Rules EVERY Pro Designer Knows](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VO1FTj4chao))

### Use mirror, blur and distance to see past reading
The brain reads text as communication, so spacing and weight errors hide. Flip the page horizontally, blur it, or view it at arm's length or small scale. Text and icons are perceived as filled masses, so judge overall visual weight, not outlines. If a viewer needs a minute of squinting to decode something, it is too hard: cut detail that fails a two-second read.
- Look for: one letter, icon or text line that looks darker or lighter than its neighbors; graphics that only work large.
Sources: willpatersondesign ([Logo Design Process #2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BLlsk3RZZ_g)); willpatersondesign ([Master the Art of Branded Typography](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-gSlKmwtEcA)); willpatersondesign ([Reviewing INSANE Subscriber LOGO Designs! Ep 16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5fx0etc14nk))

### Nudge by eye, not by the inspector
Round shapes look smaller than square ones at the same height, so they need slight overshoot. Diagonals or thin lines crossing a thick shape look misaligned (Poggendorff illusion, as cited). Bright objects look larger. When icons, circles and text sit in a row, compare zoomed out and nudge a few pixels until it looks right, even if the numbers say equal. Round letters also need to sit closer to straight ones when kerning.
Sources: willpatersondesign ([ELON MUSK Took my Advice](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X6ET8f6dbJk)); willpatersondesign ([10 MIND BLOWING Logo Design Tips 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gW5IpIUmOr8)); willpatersondesign ([How To Kern Professionally](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DwzyRNcLkao))

### Use real content in demos and prototypes
Seed screens with realistic names, currencies, dates and long strings, as Wise prefills a balance with the right currency symbol and format. Linear designs with real data so problems show early.
- Look for: truncation, number formatting and wrap failures that lorem ipsum hides.
Sources: Hatch Conference ([Henrique Gusso - Wise](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BHlWX9HeTtM)); Hatch Conference ([Adrien Griveau: How Linear designs for Planners](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9sJCu7BPvSw))

### Show the reaction, not the slide
A 20-30 second clip of a real user lighting up at a feature convinced one team more than arguing it was MVP-worthy. Record reaction clips during testing and attach them to the review. For a demo or landing page, a short real-use clip beats feature copy.
Sources: hatchconference ([Why Design Teams Need Vision More Than Ever](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qhMRwJbHmxg))

### Ask a short in-app survey to find retention drivers
Skyscanner's on-site survey asks return intent, which job the user came to do and whether they finished it, then rates attributes (ease of use, clear language, friendly tone, price accuracy, trustworthiness) plus open text. Attributes are weighted by regression and each can have an owner. Add the same shape in-app: return intent, job completed, a few attribute ratings, one open text field.
Sources: Hatch Conference ([How Design Moves Revenue, Retention, and Market Share at Skyscanner](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5yJYLrbvOYY))

## Attention: where the eye goes
### Break the pattern once, on the thing that must be seen
When a pattern is set and then broken, the eye goes to the break (surprise, Von Restorff isolation). The odd item is the one remembered: a larger pull quote, one CTA unlike everything around it. One element per screen deviates (size, color, bleed) and nothing else competes. Two deviants cancel each other.
- Look for: a screen where three things claim to be special.
- Fix: one deviant, everything else neutral.
Sources: DesignSpo ([$1,000,000 Design Portfolios](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DaeEKKSpgXU)); SatoriGraphics ([The POWERFUL Side Of Graphic Design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vH0AtTEj_LY))

### A loud headline sets the budget for everything else
When one element is huge, nothing else may be big. It must lead the eye to the supporting text and CTA, optionally with an arrow from headline to button. Keep subtitle, nav and buttons clearly smaller than a loud hero headline.
- Look for: a giant headline competing with an equally heavy nav or a second large block.
Sources: DesignSpo ([I redesigned YOUR websites](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-F1F4YISY4E))

### Remove random directions
Several shapes or edges pointing to random spots feel disorganized and uncomfortable (visual anxiety). Build one strong focal point and remove decorative diagonals, arrows or angled cards that do not support the main path.
Sources: SatoriGraphics ([Master This Design Theory & Upgrade Your Designs Now!!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D4RPBIAvq5A))

### Spend red like a scarce resource
SatoriGraphics cites: K.W. Jacobs 1974 found red more stimulating than green, and green more than blue, and that a person in a red room has raised awareness and heart rate. A 1981 study (named in the captions as Richard Cooler) is cited as showing color affects heart rate readings and the emotional perception of objects. Red sits first on the attention-order chart. Use red or orange for short alerts, errors and destructive actions, and calm low-saturation blues and greens for trust and long reading areas. Do not flood an app in red, and do not use it as a decorative accent. A little red gives depth and focus, a lot is noise.
Sources: SatoriGraphics ([The ONLY Colour Theory Video You Ever Need To Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HKtTNOgfhAU)); SatoriGraphics ([LESS Than 10% Of Designers Know This](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dGYCn9qYjUQ)); willpatersondesign ([How To Create STUNNING Layouts](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9JiKfJdXZWU))

### Withhold something in the hero
Of two good posts, the one that tells everything at once is easy to ignore, and the one that holds something back makes people pause. Over-clean, obvious templates read as generic. Lead with one intriguing line or visual and reveal details further down.
Sources: SatoriGraphics ([How To Train Your Graphic Design Eye To Beat AI in 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-KIWKgxu8cw))

## Memory and effort limits
### Chunk to 3-4 items, not a long list of reasons
Working memory holds about 3 or 4 things (a phone number is chunked in blocks). A long pile of benefits (free shipping, free returns, warranty, loyalty) is half forgotten before the visitor reaches the end. SelfMadeWebDesigner cites this; SatoriGraphics cites 3-5 chunks at a glance. Group features into 3-4 labeled categories, keep any benefit strip to about 3-4 items, keep dashboards to a few key metrics above the fold, and group long forms into steps of a few fields.
- Look for: a single flat list of 8+ benefits, or a dashboard with 12 equal-weight tiles.
Sources: SelfMadeWebDesigner ([The Psychology of a PERFECT Website](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d-IaU9qcDGg)); SatoriGraphics ([The POWERFUL Side Of Graphic Design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vH0AtTEj_LY))

### Fewer choices convert more
TimGabe and uxpeak cite: the Columbia confectionery/jam experiment, 24 flavors led to 3% purchase and 6 flavors to 30%. TimGabe cites Netflix: thousands of titles led to 30-minute browsing and quitting, so it built personalization (over 1,300 micro-genres and about 80% of viewing from recommendations). Reduce visible options, default to the most likely one, personalize the order, and recommend one. A "recommended" row on top of a huge catalog is not a fix. Collapse the rest behind "more" or progressive disclosure.
- Look for: pricing with 5+ tiers, onboarding that asks 6 preference questions, settings that show every option at once.
Sources: TimGabe ([Give Me 10 Minutes & I'll Boost Your Website Conversions](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TIfiy1fodDs)); uxpeak ([The UX Psychology Behind Apps People Can't Stop Using](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2TlIg3VokY8)); TimGabe ([How Netflix Made Us All Binge](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hIm3d3JAsPw)); SatoriGraphics ([The POWERFUL Side Of Graphic Design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vH0AtTEj_LY))

### Ask the easy question first
Evaluative ease: the less work a decision needs, the more likely it happens. Commitment consistency: if the user first commits to the destination (the big goal), the remaining decision shrinks to "which car". Order flows so the large goal is chosen first and details second, and make each question one the brain can answer quickly.
Sources: uxpeak ([Top 3 UX/UI Redesigns That Make You Design Like a Pro](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zr37ibqXl1U))

### Reveal complexity in steps
Break a complicated task into simple steps that increase in complexity and people stay focused. Reveal advanced settings only after the basics, and explain a workflow in digestible sections. Add a short worked example, diagram or screenshot for multi-step features.
Sources: SatoriGraphics ([The POWERFUL Side Of Graphic Design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vH0AtTEj_LY))

### Build for two reads: headline, then detail
Busy visitors read only the big text. Give a tweet-length version first (large headings carry the message) and put details behind it for those who want more. When torn between a flashy and a clear option, pick the one a skimmer understands in a glance.
Sources: SelfMadeWebDesigner ([How to Build a UX Design Portfolio that Get's You Hired](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XDPZOqMoS9g)); SelfMadeWebDesigner ([50 Web Design Tips & Tricks](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rRFD6tENX2U))

### Show a worked example or image instead of bullets
hatchconference speakers cite: reading bullets activates only language processing, while a story also activates experience areas of the brain. A second speaker cites that showing an image of a circle gives about six times the recall of showing the word. SatoriGraphics cites the picture superiority effect: how type looks sticks more than what it says. Use a demo, screenshot or short example in the hero instead of a feature list, and keep the same type treatment on key pages.
Sources: hatchconference ([The Danger of Gaming the System](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_m2T86qycPA)); hatchconference ([Loom's VP of Design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aH3GHVnL7YU)); SatoriGraphics ([The Key To MASTERING Typography](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MW0P0JC1EIs))

### Translate numbers users cannot judge
People cannot interpret "10 kg CO2" but understand "10 euros". Compare an abstract figure with something relatable and frame feedback positively, since negative framing puts people off. For any unfamiliar metric (tokens, GB, latency), add a relatable comparison or a delta against the previous period ("12 percent lower than last week"). Netflix's "97% match" and human category names ("Because you watched...") follow the same logic.
Sources: hatchconference ([Creating product experiences for a sustainable future](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=noDIQCuHv2g)); TimGabe ([How Netflix Made Us All Binge](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hIm3d3JAsPw))

### Weigh every feature against the effort it asks for
Levitt: people want the hole, not the drill. 3D TVs failed because glasses, special content and cost were not worth the small gain. Before adding a toggle, wizard step or flow, state the outcome it serves and the extra effort it costs. Score a screen on three pillars: effectiveness (does it work), efficiency (is it easy), satisfaction (does it feel good), since satisfaction is the differentiator when features are cheap to copy. Scan for any moment that needs a pause, such as a vague label or no obvious primary action.
Sources: amirmoradiUX ([How to Become a Product Designer in the AI Era](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ky_F3jdnT4Y)); amirmoradiUX ([Why UX Design Became the Most Important Skill in Tech](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C76MGVAAYec))

### Design for outcomes, not screens
Ask for each flow: what is the fastest, least distracting line to the result? Separate mechanical, repetitive, rule-based steps (automate or remove) from emotional or judgement steps (where the experience is the value). Replace mechanical forms with defaults or AI suggestions. Keep info-dense data views visible, with automation summarizing trends and flagging anomalies. Intercom's Fin prices per resolved ticket, aligned to outcome.
Sources: TimGabe ([The Future of App Design is Invisible](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zRUoPIwCxfw))

## Trust and first impressions
### Meet the user's emotional starting point
A charity visitor is cautious and looking for trust, a fashion browser is relaxed. If the design does not meet that state, it feels off immediately. Billing, security and onboarding screens: assume anxious users and use calm color, plain language and reassurance. Marketing pages for browsers can be bolder. Pull audience data (analytics, interviews) before choosing brand colors and justify each with an audience reason. SatoriGraphics cites a study that 78% of people engaging with Cadbury on Facebook were women, and the brand's purple signals luxury and a feminine tone.
Sources: SatoriGraphics ([Change How You Think As A Designer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4PHzHgDjiu4)); SatoriGraphics ([The ONLY Colour Theory Video You Ever Need To Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HKtTNOgfhAU))

### Audit in the order: safe, excited, justified
Visitors act as three voices. The safety and familiarity voice decides first, the emotion voice wants delight, and the logic voice looks for reasons to justify a decision already made. On a landing or pricing page ask in order: does it look familiar and trustworthy at first glance, is there something engaging, and are the rational reasons (proof, guarantees, specifics) easy to find afterward.
Sources: SelfMadeWebDesigner ([The Psychology of a PERFECT Website](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d-IaU9qcDGg))

### Keep the skeleton standard, put surprises inside it
Visitors arrive with mental models from every other site. If something is not where the model says, people do not search, they leave. SelfMadeWebDesigner cites a gaming site that moved its mobile menu to the bottom for thumb reach and had to revert because nobody found it. Do not relocate standard navigation for a "better ergonomics" argument. Safe-only design bores and novel-only scares ("most advanced yet acceptable"), so add 1-3 restrained delights (an unusual hover response, a small success animation) on a conventional layout. If a delight is too strong it triggers the "unsafe" reaction.
- Look for: a delight that shifts layout or blocks the task.
Sources: SelfMadeWebDesigner ([The Psychology of a PERFECT Website](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d-IaU9qcDGg))

### Wrap smart features in a familiar pattern
Spotify's Discover Weekly looks like any other playlist, with the same play buttons, while hiding a heavy recommendation engine. Pandora made users rate songs, which felt like homework. Do not make the tech the hero. Put AI or smart features inside existing patterns (search bar, list, inbox) instead of a new AI surface users must learn.
Sources: TimGabe ([Viral Design Tricks from Spotify](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tpg0pxKHrCA))

### Offer trust in levels
Users range from "give the AI control of my computer" power users, to newcomers using it as a search box, to skeptics. Default to simple, safe behavior and reveal advanced control (permissions, memory, integrations) in settings. State what the product reads and writes, and ask before acting on the user's behalf.
Sources: Panel ([AI is Redesigning Design Tools](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Rrt_MDrpraU))

### Put proof where the decision happens
Show trust scores and testimonials near the hero CTA and pricing, with real names and photos. SatoriGraphics notes sites proudly show Trustpilot scores on the homepage.
Sources: SatoriGraphics ([The POWERFUL Side Of Graphic Design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vH0AtTEj_LY))

### Make the target pricing plan the center
Users often pick the middle of three. Make the middle plan taller, brighter and bolder (example $15 / $25 / $50), with a ring, a "Most popular" badge and a filled CTA while the others are outlined. The speaker says it will not work on everyone but draws more clicks. One speaker also cites a price effect (not named) that people value things more when they pay for them, so avoid giving the core product away with no price signal.
Sources: SatoriGraphics ([The POWERFUL Side Of Graphic Design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vH0AtTEj_LY)); willpatersondesign ([4 Things I Wish I Knew When I Started Logo Design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kZ7phYnPxk4))

### Consistency builds familiarity and costs switching
In crowded markets users switch for feelings, not features, and leaving means relearning habits. Audit that the same action has the same verb, placement and confirmation pattern everywhere. Skyscanner cites internal results: fixing inconsistencies across feature, ergonomic and stylistic layers produced millions in uplift from about 20% of fixes, with stylistic fixes about a third of the total. Say "consistency" to stakeholders, not "design quality", and A/B test fixes where possible. Repeated exposure to one simple mark and UI language builds recognition, so do not rotate styles between screens.
Sources: TimGabe ([Viral Design Tricks from Spotify](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tpg0pxKHrCA)); Hatch Conference ([Skyscanner](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5yJYLrbvOYY)); willpatersondesign ([Reviewing INSANE Subscriber LOGO Designs! Ep 17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V0ye52TRGYo))

### Visible care is forgiven for
People sense when someone tried hard. If a user senses love in an interface they forgive more and keep it. Polish microcopy, empty states, hover states and alignment where users touch most. Review two layers: does the first screen make me want to click, and does it still feel good after weeks (speed, reliability, consistent details).
Sources: hatchconference ([Erik Spiekermann - The Hand is the Window on to the Mind](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hJhH4ZgsBhI))

## Form, shape and color as signals
### Match shape language to the personality
Spiky and angular reads sharp or energetic, round reads soft and friendly. Natalia Filvarova cites the kiki/boba effect (across cultures people name the spiky shape "kiki" and the round one "boba"). Round, friendly typefaces can build trust while staying professional (a friendlier redesign was more trusted than the old one). Rounded containers and rounded sans for friendly consumer or onboarding, sharper corners and firmer sans for finance or security. Check illustration and copy tone agree. Treat this as a light signal.
Sources: Natalia Filvarova ([Embrace Multisensory & Accessible Design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OjlSIq29YzM)); willpatersondesign ([Reviewing STRANGE Subscriber LOGO Designs Ep 19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c6mJJvaE0WY)); SatoriGraphics ([I Redesigned 3 Famous Brands In 1 Day](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2XMdwxZ2GCk))

### Use lines and repetition to set mood
Horizontal lines feel calm, vertical feel dignified, formal and strong, diagonals give action, curves give graceful movement. Calm settings pages: horizontal bands and wide cards. Formal finance: vertical structure. Symmetry and repeated patterns (card grids, equal gutters) read as order and calm, but break symmetry deliberately with a clear anchor.
Sources: SatoriGraphics ([Control A Viewers Eye For BETTER Graphic Designs](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dTsWYQawXRw)); willpatersondesign ([Logo Hack: The Trick That Changed Everything](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kVLw_eZNhyw))

### Simple marks trigger emotion, complex ones trigger thought
SatoriGraphics says simple logos make people react emotionally while intricate ones make them think, and brands want emotion for affinity. Marks should be memorable, legible at a glance and obviously related to the audience. Simplified marks stay in memory and gain familiarity with exposure. Most iconic logos became iconic through heavy marketing, not intrinsic quality.
Sources: SatoriGraphics ([4 Ways To Improve Your LOGO Designs RIGHT NOW](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FcJtrfTBF_M)); willpatersondesign ([Reviewing INSANE Subscriber LOGO Re-Designs! Ep 30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZxxPwsKDJRc))

## Peak, end and completion
### Design the ending, not just the task
TimGabe cites Kahneman: memory of an experience is mostly its most intense moment and its end, and cites Disney reportedly seeing about 70% of visitors return by managing peaks and the final moment. Many apps just end. After the main task show a closing state with a summary and a gentle next step (a checkmark, summary card, "you showed up today"), not a dead screen or an instant redirect. Ask for ratings or feedback right after success, as Uber does with rating and tip, not before value is delivered.
Sources: TimGabe ([The Tiny Design Rule Behind Top 1% Apps](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=11h4H3TAGkM))

### Use the post-conversion moment
After conversion there is less fight about content and more room for awareness. On the success or receipt screen after checkout, upgrade or task completion, show a rewarding summary of what was achieved plus a share or next-step action. A bike-share app shows saved carbon after parking with a share action.
Sources: hatchconference ([Creating product experiences for a sustainable future](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=noDIQCuHv2g))

### Amplify the single strongest emotional moment
Find where users feel success, relief or surprise and add delight there: a milestone animation, a first-success celebration, friendly error text, a screenshot-worthy moment. Do not spread delight evenly. Delight opens the door and depth keeps the user (Sennett's spark versus lived experience as cited), so without the spark nobody starts and without depth they leave.
Sources: TimGabe ([How Netflix Made Us All Binge](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hIm3d3JAsPw)); hatchconference ([Erik Spiekermann](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hJhH4ZgsBhI))

### Stage a reveal in three steps
Pack openings run anticipation (tap, unknown contents), reveal (cards flip one at a time, each resetting anticipation) and celebration (glow, haptics). For a report-ready or AI-result moment, stage it in steps instead of dumping everything at once.
Sources: TimGabe ([I Studied 500+ Gamified Apps](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LXX_qOA5D8E))

### Show open loops, but only a few
Zeigarnik-style effect: people remember unfinished tasks and want to close them. Show progress visually and keep the next goal close. TimGabe cites Apple's activity rings: a 90% filled circle is an open loop the brain wants to close (Gestalt closure), with a cited 49.5% behavior change in 160,000 people. Use partially filled rings or bars on setup checklists ("2 of 3 done") with a next-step prompt, never 20 loops on one screen. Celebrate real milestones (a personal record, 100 rides = 100 rides badge), not app opens.
Sources: TimGabe ([How Top 1% Apps Keep You Hooked](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D9SXaj6qQIQ)); TimGabe ([I Studied 500+ Gamified Apps](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LXX_qOA5D8E))

## Habit, investment and identity
### Tie the habit to a cue the user already has
TimGabe cites a 2015 University of Bristol study: neither rewards nor reminders built lasting habits, while habits tied to something already automatic (after brushing teeth, sitting at the desk) kept working. A cited Yale study says habit formation takes 18 to 254 days, so do not switch to nagging after month one. Onboarding should ask "when will you do this?" and bundle tasks into one daily routine and one reminder.
Sources: TimGabe ([Retention 101: How Top 1% Apps Keep Users for Years](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IKrLPhaFcp4))

### Design the day after a miss
A streak that survives the worst day keeps users. Duolingo's streak freeze (doubling freezes from one to two gave +0.38% in daily active learners per the speaker), vacation mode, moving a missed credit to the day completed, pause and preserve. TimGabe cites that streaks shift from "I want to" to "I can't miss today" over time and that Snapchat research links streaks to FOMO. Give any streak or goal a choice of level, a pause, skip or catch-up, and supportive copy after a miss. Never an unescapable loss.
Sources: TimGabe ([Retention 101](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IKrLPhaFcp4)); TimGabe ([I Studied 500+ Gamified Apps](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LXX_qOA5D8E))

### Let users invest, and show the deposit
TimGabe cites the IKEA effect study (Norton, Mochon, Ariely): people assign 63% more value to things they helped create. Midjourney personalization is cited as adopted by over 70% of active users within 3 months. Let users customize early (name a workspace, pin favorites, build a dashboard). Make each session deposit something that compounds and cannot be exported (personalization, trained rules, integrations), and show it ("tracked for 1,095 nights", "your setup so far"). Automate the deposit so users invest without thinking. Compare a 6-month user's experience with a 10-day user's.
Sources: TimGabe ([The New Way Apps Dominate in 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yYs7iv81Ppk)); SatoriGraphics ([The POWERFUL Side Of Graphic Design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vH0AtTEj_LY))

### Put the stake on screen, not the metric
Forest shows a tree that dies if you leave, so the pull becomes "I don't want to kill it". Make the thing that would be lost the largest element on a habit or focus screen, with the timer secondary. TimGabe cites that losses hurt about twice as much as equal gains, so show cumulative totals that never reset (total classes, total exports) and tiers that unlock tangible value, with freezes that must be earned. Cited: Peloton at 90% annual subscriber retention, driven by community and never-capping cumulative metrics.
Sources: TimGabe ([How Top 1% Indie Apps Beats Giants](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MAvKHToGiVY)); TimGabe ([How To Scientifically Design Addictive Apps](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yBpv5rZoBjA)); TimGabe ([Why Irresistible Apps Are Designed This Way](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VkFDdGGGA-s))

### Give the core object its own clock
FarmVille crops rot after a timer, Hinge Standouts reset every 24 hours, Life Reset has a countdown to the next boss fight. Something changes on a timer whether the user watches or not, which creates an open loop and loss aversion. Reminders alone are not the mechanism. Ask whether the core object can age, expire or refresh on a schedule (weekly digest, daily batch) and show the countdown. Do it ethically.
Sources: TimGabe ([Why Irresistible Apps Are Designed This Way](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VkFDdGGGA-s))

### Show identity, not activity
"You completed 10 tasks" and "power user" feel hollow. Spotify Wrapped shows personal traits (top genres, favorite artist) so the share says "this is so me". Write "you are a night owl who does your best work after 9pm", not "25 tasks done". Give a flattering, specific, earned label (a type, status or role) that appears on the profile and share cards, since people do not churn from their own identity. It only works if users are already attached.
Sources: TimGabe ([Viral Design Tricks from Spotify](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tpg0pxKHrCA)); TimGabe ([The Twisted Psychology Behind Top 1% Apps](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nQKWYmrixCU))

### Make bragging and growth the same action
Find something the product knows that users are proud of (weekly recap, run map, word grid) and package it as a clean card (OG image) with the product name on it. Tie the full version to inviting friends so sharing is acquisition. A referral program bolted onto something nobody wants to show off does nothing.
Sources: TimGabe ([The Twisted Psychology Behind Top 1% Apps](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nQKWYmrixCU))

### Make competition winnable and local
Strava runs thousands of micro leaderboards (segments by road and age or gender cohort) instead of one global board. TimGabe cites winnability as the strongest predictor of competitive motivation. Scope leaderboards to team, week or cohort so most users can rank. Frame metrics as a mirror ("how do I compare") rather than a report, and only where visibility fits the product. TimGabe cites Strava deleting 3.9M gamed activities from leaderboards.
Sources: TimGabe ([I Studied 500+ Gamified Apps](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LXX_qOA5D8E)); TimGabe ([How To Scientifically Design Addictive Apps](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yBpv5rZoBjA))

### Make shared presence visible, with consent
Ladder pins thousands of members to the same workout on the same day, and a cohort calendar makes your absence visible so skipping becomes social. Add shared-state signals (team activity, same-day cohort) where the product is used alone today. Use sparingly. Tinder's double-blind match hid rejection (cited: 26 million matches a day, 1.6 billion swipes per day), so avoid read receipts, "viewed by" lists and visible declines unless needed.
Sources: TimGabe ([The Weird Design Playbook of 6 App Outliers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HA4yB_sd62Q)); TimGabe ([How Tinder's Design Hacked Society](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4yKtlOWClhA))

### Let features unlock each other
For each feature ask whether it unlocks something else or sits waiting. Raycast's search bar gains power with each extension, a store gives reasons to explore, and an API feeds the store. Suggest the next capability right after a relevant action. Meet users in tools they already use: Raycast integrated with GitHub, Jira and Slack (open assigned tickets, update Jira without a browser), so teammates found it where they already worked. Offer real actions, not just a logo.
Sources: TimGabe ([How Raycast Reached a $100M+ Valuation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eWGNjTLK84c))

### Pick one opinion and over-serve one user
Raycast is keyboard-first on purpose, minimal on screen with depth revealed progressively, and has a rigid open, type, act model. Every decision passed one test: does it improve speed, reduce cognitive load, or fit the keyboard workflow. Against Alfred it chose a larger central search bar, fewer options and better defaults. Strong opinions repel some users and create the strongest fans. Write the product's one test sentence, reject features that fail it, and state the opinion in the UI and docs.
Sources: TimGabe ([How Raycast Reached a $100M+ Valuation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eWGNjTLK84c))

### Check where the emotional layer sits against AI agents
TimGabe argues that if an agent can do the task by text message, the reason to open the app must be identity, community or ritual. Strava puts social activity as the home feed, and Whoop turns data into one weekly identity number (Pace of Aging, -1 to 3). His test: where does the social layer sit in the navigation, and if it is buried or absent you are exposed. Cited: Gartner says 40% of enterprise apps will have task-specific agents by end of year. Treat as a strategic prompt, not a rule for every utility tool (see the disagreement below).
Sources: TimGabe ([Why Most SaaS Companies Will Die in 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZXWUg1e1PAs))

## Ethics: engagement versus the user's time
### Do not copy slot-machine patterns in utility products
Pull-to-refresh is described as a slot machine and endless feeds mixing mediocre with great as a roulette table. TimGabe cites Skinner: variable-ratio rewards drive the most compulsive behavior, and he calls these mechanisms morally complicated. In a productivity or B2B SaaS avoid variable-reward loops, infinite scroll and manufactured streaks, and prefer clear endpoints and "done" states. If you use any surprise, keep most of the reward system predictable and transparent, with one visible measure of total progress instead of 20 scattered badges.
Sources: Hatch Conference ([Samuel Huber - Prototyping for the Planet](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Bsvw9hm3FtY)); TimGabe ([How To Scientifically Design Addictive Apps](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yBpv5rZoBjA))

### Add at most one gamification mechanic
TimGabe cites: LinkedIn retired Top Voice badges in 2024 (quantity over quality), Foursquare dropped badges and mayorships in 2014, Google News dropped its badges. A 2025 Frontiers in Psychology study is cited as finding an S-curve: more gamification features help to a point, then reverse engagement, and a Habitica study found 100% of participants had counterproductive effects. When asked for points, badges or streaks, add one mechanic tied to real skill progress.
Sources: TimGabe ([I Studied 500+ Gamified Apps](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LXX_qOA5D8E))

### Ask what behavior you want before "more engagement"
Forcing daily return (infinite scroll, streak bait) ignores where the product fits the user's routine. The speaker cites that people have only about four to six hours of focused attention a day, so every app cannot own it. When asked to increase engagement, first ask what behavior change or value delivery is needed. Prefer reminders and flows tied to real tasks over compulsion loops.
Sources: hatchconference ([The Danger of Gaming the System](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_m2T86qycPA))

### In utility tools, design for less time in the app
Mews, citing Don Norman, argues the user should touch the product only to make a decision or take an action, driven by smart notifications and insights, with tedious work automated. Remove steps that can be automated, default smartly, notify only when a decision is needed, and measure time to finish, not time spent in app.
Sources: Hatch Conference ([The Path to zero Screens - Juan Hidalgo](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fb7XJo0EyaE))

## Where designers disagree
### Rule of 3-5 chunks versus 7 plus or minus 2
frontend-design caps visible choices at roughly 7 (Miller, 4 to 7 chunks). SatoriGraphics cites 3 to 5 chunks at a glance, and SelfMadeWebDesigner cites 3 to 4 items in working memory. Use 7 as the ceiling for scannable lists such as nav or a menu, and 3 to 4 for things that must be remembered or compared at once (benefit strips, pricing tiers, headline metrics).

### Delight, funny errors and animation versus stillness
TimGabe advises milestone animations, funny error copy and screenshot-worthy moments. frontend-design defaults to stillness, bans "oops" in errors and loops of micro-animations. Compromise that fits both: celebrate a real milestone once with a restrained animation, and keep error text plain and instructional. SelfMadeWebDesigner also suggests an image that scales slightly on scroll and unusual hover responses, which touches frontend-design's scroll-effect bans, so use hover only.

### Engagement mechanics versus designing for disengagement
TimGabe teaches streaks, loss aversion, variable rewards, own-clock objects and social visibility as retention tools, and says the emotional layer is the moat against agents. Mews, Hatch speakers and amirmoradiUX argue utility tools should minimize screen time and avoid compulsion loops. For B2B or productivity SaaS default to the second view and borrow only forgiving, progress-based mechanics (cumulative totals, forgiving goals, a good ending). Use identity and social layers only where users already want to show or compare their work.

### Shape and line direction as emotion
SatoriGraphics suggests diagonal dividers for energetic heroes, rounded containers for community products and rounded type for friendly trust. frontend-design bans pills on cards and containers and wants a utilitarian register in Operate mode. Treat these as light signals for Persuade surfaces only, with radius inside frontend-design's 8 to 12px card limit.
